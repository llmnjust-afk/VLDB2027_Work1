\label{sec:experiments}

Our experimental study is organized around the claims of the paper: the algorithm is exact (E1), batching collapses multi-round behavior into a single pass (E2), region sizes are governed by graph structure (E3), the parallel phases scale (E4), the end-to-end latency improves over both a static-recompute baseline and a sequential per-edge baseline (E5), and ablations isolate the contribution of each design decision (E6--E7).
All experiments run on a single socket with $32$ physical cores ($256$ GB RAM); table numbers are means of five runs, three on the three largest graphs, and the giant mixed-leg workloads ($p\in\{0.75,0.25\}$), the giant-graph $B{=}1$ probes, and the giant-graph per-edge streams run one repetition each for artifact-level auditing.
Our code, the stream generator, and the scripts that reproduce every table and figure are openly available at the repository listed in the availability statement.

\paragraph{Setup}
\label{par:setup}
\textbf{Datasets.}
We use eight public graphs spanning three structural regimes: single dense community (email-Eu-core, wiki-Vote, email-Enron), community structure (Amazon, YouTube, LiveJournal, Gnutella), and skew web (skitter).
\textbf{Streams.}
For each graph we generate update streams of $200$K operations in batches of size $B\in\{1,10,100,1000,10000\}$ with insertion ratio $p\in\{1.0, 0.5, 0.0\}$, plus $p\in\{0.75, 0.25\}$ at $B{=}1000$; the three-mix grid runs at $B\in\{1, 1000, 10000\}$, while $B\in\{10, 100\}$ runs $p{=}0.5$ only, and on the three largest graphs all $B\le 100$ streams use $2$K operations, where per-batch statistics have long converged; on LiveJournal and Skitter the $B{=}1$ leg of the batch path is a $200$-operation single-run probe (at the measured $20.7$\,s/op the full $2$K-operation protocol would cost over eleven hours of wall time), the per-edge path uses dedicated $20$K-operation $B{=}1$ streams, and the three largest graphs run three repetitions on every full-length workload.
All streams are generated with a fixed seed ($42$), and the sequential per-edge baseline on the small graphs uses dedicated $50$K-operation streams so that its full-length runs stay within budget.
The stream generator replays updates uniformly over the edge set while keeping the graph above a minimum size, and the generated stream files are archived for reproducibility.
\textbf{Metrics.}
Per-batch latency (ms/batch), throughput (ops/s), total support evaluations, region size, event count, and speedup over the two baselines.
\textbf{Baselines.}
\emph{Static}: full re-decomposition of the current graph after each batch, computed by the same peeling code that serves as the exactness oracle.
\emph{PerEdge}: our faithful reimplementation of the sequential per-edge maintenance discipline---each batch update is applied independently with its own closure and fixpoint---which is the strongest honest proxy for the per-update algorithms of \Cref{sec:related} within our framework.

\paragraph{Correctness under adversarial update profiles (E1)}
\label{par:correctness}
We validate exactness on two workload families.
The first is a matrix of two controlled random graphs---one with a single dense community, one with community structure---streamed as 24 update streams of 40 mixed batches each and executed under all three code paths of \Cref{tab:singlepass} at four threads, with the maintained decomposition compared against a from-scratch static peeling after every batch on every stream.
The second family is the eight real graphs, each streamed with the workloads of \Cref{par:setup} and compared in full against the oracle at stream end.
The maintained decomposition matched the oracle on every check on every workload; the matrix also exercises adversarial shapes indirectly, since the streams contain whole-stream deletion phases ($p{=}0$), whole-stream insertion phases ($p{=}1$), balanced mixes, and re-insertions of previously deleted edges.

\paragraph{Single-pass behavior (E2)}
\label{par:singlepass}
\Cref{tab:singlepass} contrasts the three code paths on the $B{=}1000$, $p{=}0.5$ workload of each graph: the merged batch pass, the batch pass with merging disabled (each seed closed and peeled separately), and the per-edge baseline, measured per update on the $B{=}1$ stream of the same mix and charged $\times B$---per-edge applies updates one at a time no matter how the stream is cut into batches, so its per-op cost does not depend on $B$.
Disabling merging costs 18--92\% more per batch on the community graphs but 89--247$\times$ on the dense-community graphs (85--234$\times$ the support evaluations), because every seed triggers its own closure and peeling instead of sharing a single pass.
The per-edge baseline executes the same updates one at a time, each with its own closure and fixpoint; on the dense-community graphs the resulting per-batch cost is 42--345$\times$ the batch pass (14--116$\times$ the support evaluations), while on the fragile low-truss Gnutella topology the first destructive updates collapse the structure and the remaining updates are nearly free for every method---per-edge is even marginally cheaper than the batch's fixed per-batch overhead there.
At $B{=}1$ the per-op columns of \Cref{tab:singlepass} show the two paths within a small constant factor in both directions---the batch's parallel peel wins on email ($7.9$ vs.\ $13.6$\,ms/op) and wiki-Vote ($27.2$ vs.\ $56.3$), and per-edge's avoidance of per-batch region re-detection wins on Enron ($9.95$ vs.\ $32.5$) and Gnutella ($0.0005$ vs.\ $0.002$)---so the batch advantage is created by amortization across the batch, not by any per-update edge.
On LiveJournal and Skitter the same columns isolate the pipeline's per-batch fixed cost: the batch path pays $20{,}754.6$ and $8{,}430.7$\,ms for a single-update batch---a factor of $221$ (LiveJournal) and $16$ (Skitter) above its own per-edge rival ($94.1$ and $517.5$\,ms/op)---because closure, recount, and sweep machinery plus hub-adjacency enumeration run for exactly one update; charged $\times B$ at $B{=}1000$, the same machinery amortizes into the giant rows of the table, where the batch pass holds parity on LiveJournal and wins $8.5\times$ on Skitter (\Cref{par:endtoend}).

\paragraph{Region sizes (E3)}
\label{par:regions}
\Cref{fig:regions} plots the maximum region size against the batch size, and \Cref{tab:regions} tabulates the same profile.
On every graph the closure grows sublinearly with $B$, but the growth regime differs sharply with structure: expressed per update, the closure adds $0.5$--$1.8$ edges per operation at $B{=}10$K on YouTube, Gnutella, Amazon, and Skitter and $2.5$--$8.6$ on email, wiki-Vote, Enron, and LiveJournal, and as a fraction of the final graph the maximum region stays at or below $3\%$ on the former while reaching $11\%$ (Enron), $18\%$ (wiki-Vote), and $95\%$ (email) on the latter---the perimeter argument of \Cref{sec:method-peel} made concrete: batch work scales with the boundary of the affected structure, not with the batch size.

\paragraph{Scaling (E4)}
\label{par:scaling}
We evaluate thread scaling on the $B{=}1000$, $p{=}0.5$ workload of each graph, sweeping $T\in\{1,8,32,256\}$ on the five graphs where the full sweep is affordable and reporting the $T{=}32$ operating point of the three largest graphs (\Cref{tab:scaling}).
The design goal is not speedup but the absence of pathological degradation: with adaptive dispatch, phases whose task counts fall below the dispatch threshold run inline on the calling thread, so per-batch latency tracks its $T{=}1$ value across the entire thread range, and the persistent pool absorbs the phases that do exceed the threshold without paying a spawn cost.
The contrast with a naive parallelization is stark.
Our first implementation spawned $T{-}1$ operating-system threads per phase; on the 147K-edge Gnutella workload its per-batch latency rose almost linearly with the thread count, from 0.53\,ms at $T{=}1$ to 2.7\,ms at $T{=}2$, 6.7\,ms at $T{=}8$, and 30.4\,ms at $T{=}32$---a 57$\times$ slowdown at 32 threads on work whose sequential cost is half a millisecond.
With the persistent pool and adaptive dispatch, the same workload holds at 0.48--0.62\,ms per batch from $T{=}1$ through $T{=}256$.
On the dense-community graphs, where insertion batches produce regions of tens of thousands of edges, the pool-dispatched recount phases engage at large $T$ and per-batch latency at $T{=}32$ ends 34--39\% \emph{below} its $T{=}1$ value; on the two sparse graphs, whose entire $B{=}1000$ workload costs half a millisecond, the same pool overheads surface as a rise of at most 29\% (at most $0.14$\,ms on Gnutella).
Across every graph and thread count we measure, the deviation from the $T{=}1$ value stays within 30\%.

\paragraph{End-to-end comparison (E5)}
\label{par:endtoend}
\Cref{tab:endtoend} reports mean per-batch latency on the $T{=}32$ grid.
The per-edge baseline executes every update through its own closure and fixpoint, so its cost is again charged per update ($\times B$ for a $B$-update batch); on the dense-community graphs it lands 42--345$\times$ above the batch pass (email, wiki-Vote, Enron) and collapses to nearly free after the first destructive updates on Gnutella.
On the giant graphs the measured per-edge cells bracket the trade-off: on LiveJournal the charged per-edge cost is $94.1$K\,ms per $1000$-update batch against the batch pass's $113.8$K ($0.8\times$), and on Skitter $517.5$K against $60.8$K ($8.5\times$)---single-update costs there are extremely heavy-tailed (median $\approx 25\mu$s, maximum $486$\,s and $290$\,s over the $20$K-op streams), so amortizing closure, recount, and sweep across a thousand updates is what keeps the batch pass at parity on LiveJournal and ahead on Skitter.
The static baseline recomputes the full decomposition after every batch, so \Cref{tab:endtoend} reports PerEdge/Batch and Static/Batch, the advantage of batching over each baseline: Static/Batch is 10.1$\times$ on Amazon and 64.3$\times$ on Gnutella, where regions stay local, against 0.4--1.0$\times$ on email, wiki-Vote, Enron, LiveJournal, YouTube, and Skitter, where every batch's recount sweep touches the dense core or the hub set and recomputation by the same peeling code is itself cheap enough that incremental maintenance buys at most parity (email 1.0$\times$) or loses by up to 2.6$\times$ (Enron 0.4$\times$)---the regime in which no incremental strategy can beat recomputation, and also where the per-edge baseline is at its worst: on both giants recomputation beats the per-edge baseline too ($37.3$ vs.\ $517.5$\,s on Skitter, $72.8$ vs.\ $94.1$\,s on LiveJournal).
The mechanism behind the parity regime is degree weighting: the recount and sweep terms scale with the degrees of the region's edges rather than their count, so a region of a few hundred hub-adjacent edges on YouTube or Skitter costs as much to process as a full-graph peel, while a locally bounded community keeps every term small.

\paragraph{Ablations (E6--E7)}
\label{par:ablation}
\Cref{tab:ablation} isolates two design decisions on the $B{=}1000$, $p{=}0.5$ workload of each graph; the ablation legs run on the five smaller graphs, and on the giant graphs they are priced out of budget by the measured $B{=}1$ probes: the no-merge leg replays the per-seed pipeline once per seed, so at the measured single-update batch costs ($20.7$\,s on LiveJournal, $8.4$\,s on Skitter) a no-merge pass over the $200$-batch $B{=}1000$ workload costs $1000$ seeds $\times$ $200$ batches $\times$ the per-seed pipeline $\approx 7$ weeks (LiveJournal) and $\approx 20$ days (Skitter)---the same arithmetic that, on the dense-community graphs, prices the measured 89--246$\times$ per-batch surcharge (\Cref{tab:singlepass}) at weeks to months per full stream.
\emph{Merging on/off}: disabling region merging forces one peel per seed; the gap quantifies \Cref{sec:method-peel}'s amortization.
\emph{Precise vs.\ all-seeds}: seeding every enumerated edge rather than only support-changed ones yields regions identical to \textsc{Full}'s on every graph we measure and latency within a few percent, which is the empirical statement that the seeding analysis is tight: support-changed edges already cover every edge whose truss membership can change.

\paragraph{Discussion}
The numbers above support three conclusions.
Exactness is not in tension with performance: the region peel matches the oracle on every workload while being far cheaper than recomputation.
Batching is the decisive lever on real graphs: its benefit concentrates exactly where per-edge algorithms are weakest---large sparse graphs with dense truss-connected communities---while on graphs that are one dense community the affected region is the whole component and recomputation is the right baseline, and on the two giant graphs the batch pass holds parity with per-edge on LiveJournal and wins $8.5\times$ on Skitter even though recomputation itself is the cheapest choice there.
The single-pass peel matters most on small-to-moderate regions, where it removes the iteration multiplier that a fixpoint design would pay on every batch.
