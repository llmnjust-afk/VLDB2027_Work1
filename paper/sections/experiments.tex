\label{sec:experiments}

Our experimental study is organized around the claims of the paper: the algorithm is exact (E1), batching collapses multi-round behavior into a single pass (E2), region sizes are governed by graph structure (E3), the parallel phases scale (E4), and the end-to-end latency improves over both a static-recompute baseline and a sequential per-edge baseline (E5--E6), with ablations isolating the contribution of each design decision (E7--E8).
All experiments run on a single socket with $32$ physical cores ($256$ GB RAM); every reported number is the mean of five runs (three on the three largest graphs), with the standard deviation reported alongside.
Our code, the stream generator, and the scripts that reproduce every table and figure are openly available at the repository listed in the availability statement.

\paragraph{Setup}
\label{par:setup}
\textbf{Datasets.}
We use eight public graphs spanning three structural regimes: single dense community (email-Eu-core, wiki-Vote, email-Enron), community structure (Amazon, YouTube, LiveJournal, Gnutella), and skew web (skitter).
\textbf{Streams.}
For each graph we generate update streams of $200$K operations in batches of size $B\in\{1,10,100,1000,10000\}$ with insertion ratio $p\in\{1.0, 0.5, 0.0\}$, plus $p\in\{0.75, 0.25\}$ at $B{=}1000$; the $B{=}1$ streams use $20$K single-update batches ($2$K on the three largest graphs), where per-batch statistics have long converged, and on the three largest graphs the $B\le 10$ legs run at $p{=}0.5$ with three repetitions, the mix profiled in \Cref{par:regions}, since their single-update legs are dominated by hub-edge maintenance cost that the mix does not change.
The stream generator replays updates uniformly over the edge set while keeping the graph above a minimum size, and the generated stream files are archived for reproducibility.
\textbf{Metrics.}
Per-batch latency (ms/batch), throughput (ops/s), total support evaluations, region size, event count, and speedup over the two baselines.
\textbf{Baselines.}
\emph{Static}: full re-decomposition of the current graph after each batch, computed by the same peeling code that serves as the exactness oracle.
\emph{PerEdge}: our faithful reimplementation of the sequential per-edge maintenance discipline---each batch update is applied independently with its own closure and fixpoint---which is the strongest honest proxy for the per-update algorithms of \Cref{sec:related} within our framework.

\paragraph{Correctness under adversarial streams (E1)}
\label{par:correctness}
We validate exactness on two workload families.
The first is a matrix of two controlled random graphs---one with a single dense community, one with community structure---streamed as 24 update streams of 40 mixed batches each and executed under all three code paths of \Cref{tab:singlepass} at four threads, with the maintained decomposition compared against a from-scratch static peeling after every batch on the smallest streams, every 25th batch on the hundred-edge streams, every 50th batch on the thousand-edge streams, and at the end of every stream.
The second family is the eight real graphs, each streamed at 200K updates and compared in full against the oracle at stream end.
The maintained decomposition matched the oracle on every check on every workload; the matrix also exercises adversarial shapes indirectly, since the streams contain whole-stream deletion phases ($p{=}0$), whole-stream insertion phases ($p{=}1$), balanced mixes, and re-insertions of previously deleted edges.

\paragraph{Scaling (E4)}
\label{par:scaling}
We evaluate thread scaling on the $B{=}1000$, $p{=}0.5$ workload of each graph, sweeping the thread count and reporting $T\in\{1,8,32,256\}$ (\Cref{tab:scaling}).
The design goal is not speedup but the absence of pathological degradation: with adaptive dispatch, phases whose task counts fall below the dispatch threshold run inline on the calling thread, so per-batch latency stays within noise of its $T{=}1$ value across the entire thread range, and the persistent pool absorbs the phases that do exceed the threshold without paying a spawn cost.
The contrast with a naive parallelization is stark.
Our first implementation spawned $T{-}1$ operating-system threads per phase; on the 147K-edge Gnutella workload its per-batch latency rose almost linearly with the thread count, from 0.53\,ms at $T{=}1$ to 2.7\,ms at $T{=}2$, 6.7\,ms at $T{=}8$, and 30.4\,ms at $T{=}32$---a 57$\times$ slowdown at 32 threads on work whose sequential cost is half a millisecond.
With the persistent pool and adaptive dispatch, the same workload holds at 0.5--0.6\,ms per batch from $T{=}1$ through $T{=}256$.
On the densest graphs, where insertion batches produce regions above 90K edges, the pool-dispatched recount phases engage at large $T$; their contribution is bounded by the sequential sweep that dominates end-to-end latency, so whole-batch latency stays within XX\% of its $T{=}1$ value on every graph and thread count we measure.

\paragraph{Discussion}
The numbers above support three conclusions.
Exactness is not in tension with performance: the region peel matches the oracle on every workload while being far cheaper than recomputation.
Batching is the decisive lever on real graphs: its benefit concentrates exactly where per-edge algorithms are weakest---dense truss-connected communities---because the amortization is proportional to the region the batch would otherwise re-traverse.
The single-pass peel matters most on small-to-moderate regions, where it removes the iteration multiplier that a fixpoint design would pay on every batch.
